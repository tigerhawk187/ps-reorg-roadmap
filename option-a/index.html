The case for change

Renewance is scaling. The field organization is planned to move from roughly seventy technicians toward one hundred and twenty, and the program portfolio, MVT, LTSA at scale, remote operations, from concept to revenue, across a footprint that is already nationwide and international. That future state is not constrained by talent. It is constrained by placement. Three distinct jobs, technical program leadership, people management and resource management, sit fused inside two roles today, so the business realizes a fraction of each rather than the whole of any. This reorganization separates them and assigns each to the person whose capability matches it. The return is growth capacity and margin from a team the business already funds.

MVT, LTSA at scale, remote support and monitoring, technical readiness for regulated markets. None of these mature while the people who would build them are absorbed elsewhere.

No net-new headcount for the structure. Two compensation adjustments. One reallocation of work the business already funds.

Span of control and expansion

Structure without a stated span is a structure that drifts. Three thresholds are agreed, and they are what convert this from a one-time change into a model the business can grow inside.

How growth is absorbed

Growth is absorbed by adding associate managers under the Senior Services Manager. The performance engineering group does not need to scale with technician headcount, because its output is programs rather than coverage. Project management scales with project volume rather than with technician count. When the Senior Services Manager reaches his span, the business adds a second services manager, and regional structure becomes the question worth asking at that point rather than before it.

The planning and scheduling seat

The Resource Manager role is also a development seat. It gives whoever holds it a complete view of how the business runs day to day, which makes it a credible path toward services management. If LTSA becomes a dedicated scope it will need its own services manager, and that is a realistic destination for this role rather than a hypothetical one.

LTSA contracts carry obligations that are measured rather than asserted. Availability, capacity, round-trip efficiency and degradation are contractual quantities, and each one is either demonstrated with data or conceded. Renewance is building that capability incidentally today, inside roles whose primary work is something else, and every LTSA the business signs increases the exposure. A performance guarantee the business cannot substantiate is a liability that surfaces at the first dispute or at renewal, whichever comes first.

Performance engineering is the discipline that owns those quantities. It sets the measurement approach, holds the analytics, produces the reporting the asset owner receives, substantiates warranty and guarantee claims against the OEM, and performs root cause when a system underperforms. It is a named function in every mature LTSA organization Renewance will be measured against, and naming it does two things at once. Externally it tells owners and OEMs the capability exists. Internally it gives work that is currently scattered a single home and a single accountable owner. The business needs this for LTSA regardless of any reorganization, which is what makes it the right anchor for the role rather than a label applied to one.

Jordan Larson currently holds two jobs that draw on entirely different capabilities. He is the technical authority the organization consults, and he is the manager of the field services organization. Those two loads compete, and people management wins by default because personnel issues arrive with urgency attached. The work that would compound in value gets whatever hours remain.

The constraint on the new role is as important as its content, and it should be stated plainly: the Senior Performance Engineer carries no people or resource management of the services team. He manages his own three direct reports and nothing beyond them. He does not own planning, he does not own scheduling, he does not own allocation of technicians, and he does not adjudicate personnel matters. Those functions move in their entirety to the Senior Services Manager and the Resource Manager. A personnel question put to him is redirected, not answered, and the redirect is published so the organization expects it. This is a deliberate design constraint rather than an incidental consequence, because a technical role that retains even a partial people-management obligation will be pulled back into it the first week something goes wrong in the field.

The evidence for the placement is unusual, and it is worth stating precisely. Program development is not currently part of Jordan Larson’s role and never has been. The platforms he has built and that the business is now implementing were produced on his own initiative, unassigned, alongside a full people-management load. The business has been receiving that output for free and without having asked for it, which is the clearest available signal of where the capability sits and what it would produce if it were the job rather than the margin around the job. Nothing in this proposal asks whether he can do the work. He is already doing it in the hours the rest of the role leaves behind.

Two placements inside the pillar deserve a word. Training and SOP development moves out of HR and into performance engineering, because the judgment required is technical rather than administrative: what the market demands, which skills the field is short of, and how training is built into a new program early enough that people arrive ready. HR does not have that line of sight and should not be asked to. This does not require a director of training; it requires an owner who can direct it, which is what the role provides. The second is the dotted line to Renewance Connect. The business has capable software development without a consistent source of requirements, which produces tools built on assumption rather than need. Routing product direction through performance engineering supplies that source, and it puts the management of platform development inside the team that understands what the platform is for. The existing development process should be respected rather than compressed, and part of the role is learning it well enough to work within it.

His direct reports reduce to three, and all three are senior individual contributors rather than managers of teams: Renee Zachry as principal technical lead, Gaurav Iyer covering engineering and applied technology, and Dustin Fox owning training and SOP development. Brian Duffey sits on a dotted line for Renewance Connect. Three senior specialists working toward one objective is a supervisory load measured in hours per week, which is what makes the rest of the mandate achievable. Beyond the LTSA performance mandate the role owns the MVT program, the architecture of internal AI and process automation across projects, services, finance and HR, the technical build of remote support and the monitoring center, ticketing system selection and implementation, and the technical readiness assessment required before the business can credibly enter NERC low-to-medium impact markets. It also becomes the vetting function for new initiatives, so that programs stood up under time pressure receive a complete technical specification before they are committed rather than after.

Resource management is presently a residual activity performed by whoever has capacity, which is why it degrades first under load. Making it a named role with a named owner is the only way it becomes reliable at scale.

The role takes the full scope. It owns planning and scheduling of the technician workforce and runs the resourcing call. It owns daily verification that prevailing wage requirements have been met and that timecards are complete and correct, and it owns the follow-through when they are not. It works in close coordination with Patrick Lahlum and the HR team so that compliance is confirmed on a daily cycle rather than reconstructed from six months of records under audit pressure. It reports to Clint Dollins.

Two people can hold this seat. Julia Newhall and Justin Yurchek are interchangeable for it, and equally interchangeable for the accounts project management seat, so the pair of assignments is confirmed before scope documents issue rather than assumed here. Both already perform much of this work in practice, both are organised, and both are comfortable holding people to an obligation.

Two things make this work. The first is fit, and it holds for either candidate: careful reading of reports, effective communication with technicians, and no difficulty asking someone to close out an obligation. The second is authority, and this is the condition on which the role succeeds or fails. A resource manager without the standing to require same-day correction becomes an administrator reporting on non-compliance rather than resolving it. She must be visibly empowered by Clint Dollins and by HR at the point of announcement, not incrementally over the first quarter. Removing project management and invoicing responsibility from her plate is what creates the capacity for the role to be held properly.

The associate manager designation was created in April to build a leadership layer between the technicians and the service manager. It has not delivered that yet, and the reason is specific: the business has not been able to get the associate managers out of the field. They still carry field assignments, so the management work happens in the margins, and a layer that cannot absorb load is a layer technicians escalate past. The title was granted. The capacity to hold it was not created.

That distinction determines whether this move works, because repeating the April instruction without changing what puts them in the field produces the April result. What changes is the scheduling mechanism. Under this plan the Resource Manager, not field demand, determines associate manager availability, and each associate manager carries a protected management allocation that is scheduled rather than assumed. When a site is short, the answer is a technician or a deferral, not the manager. That has a real cost in field capacity and it should be priced honestly rather than absorbed quietly, which is why the allocation is set in Phase 0 and reviewed weekly by the Senior Services Manager instead of being left to good intentions.

This plan gives each associate manager complete ownership of their technician group. They conduct the one-on-ones. They communicate project assignments and project scope to their technicians. They are the source of direction for their technicians and the destination for their technicians' questions. They are accountable for the conduct and the compliance of their group, and they apply the defined consequence when a rule is broken rather than routing the determination upward. Clint Dollins supervises and coaches them through this transition.

Reachability is a hard requirement of the role rather than a courtesy. If a technician has a problem on a Sunday, that technician reaches their associate manager and gets a person on the phone. This has been intermittent rather than absent, and intermittent is sufficient to break the model, because a technician who cannot reach their manager once will escalate by default from then on. A published standard with a named backup for each associate manager closes it.

One open question sits inside this move. Travel booking can be held centrally by the Resource Manager, which produces consistency and a single point of cost control, or distributed to the associate managers, which reinforces their ownership of their technicians. Both are defensible. The recommendation is central booking under the Resource Manager with associate managers owning the travel plan, which separates the decision about who travels where from the transaction of booking it.

The most persistent source of friction between the two organizations is that project managers communicate directly with technicians for status and, at times, for direction. This produces duplicated instruction, contradicts the service manager, and consumes project manager hours that belong to the customer and the budget. It also creates a relationship that does not work in practice, because most technicians do not know the project managers, while they do know their own manager.

The rule is straightforward. Project managers obtain status from the service manager, the associate manager or the technical lead accountable for that site. Project managers do not obtain status from technicians and do not direct technicians. Technicians take direction from their associate manager. Where a technician is at a site, the project manager speaks to whoever manages that site from the services side, not to the technician.

Inside that boundary, the project manager scope is budget, schedule, customer and vendor communication, invoicing and purchase approval. Every project carries a communication plan without exception for size. Project managers own all invoicing and all purchase approval for their projects, which is what makes budget accountability real rather than nominal.

Tae Wills becomes Senior Project Manager, which is the role as it was represented to him during hiring. He retains the decommissioning portfolio, which is among the more demanding project types the business runs and which he has managed successfully, and he holds senior oversight of the LTSA program. His coaching mandate is scope discipline. A project manager who came up through the field will gravitate toward what the technicians are doing, because that is the work he understands. Redirecting that instinct toward budget, schedule and customer is the specific developmental task, and it is more effectively performed by a senior peer than by a director.

The project management workload is rebalanced at the same time. The project portfolio currently sitting with the resourcing seat is redistributed across the four project managers rather than landing on one, with the OEM flow work moving to Zoe Thomas and Lily Hsieh and the remainder split between Declan Hunt and whichever of Justin Yurchek or Julia Newhall holds a project seat. Justin Yurchek also currently carries prevailing wage tracking and the Procore application, and those transition as part of the same rebalance.

Two project managers have been carrying hours beyond what the work should require, and in both cases the cause is the same: time spent on resourcing and personnel matters that are not in scope. Tightening the scope is expected to return that capacity rather than require additional support. Alongside that, the development plan for the junior project managers is concrete rather than aspirational: mentorship from the Senior Project Manager, a set of internal project management fundamentals assembled from existing material, and a responsibility matrix that states plainly what sits inside the role and what does not.

The second project management seat, covering the accounts that transfer out of resourcing, is taken by whichever of Justin Yurchek or Julia Newhall does not take the Resource Manager role. The program is young and will be learned rather than inherited, and he is organized and effective with customers. The technical one-off scope he currently carries, lift management, Renewance Connect items and assorted technical requests, transfers to Jordan Larson, where it belongs and where it feeds the solution development pipeline. Zoe Thomas and Lily Hsieh together take the full OEM flow portfolio rather than CATL alone, which reflects the actual shape of the work: CATL volume has been declining while Sungrow is increasing and Clou and other vendors are entering the mix.

Decision rights

A rule that carries a defined consequence does not require a discussion. It requires a determination and a record. The operative test is two questions. Did the individual do it, yes or no. Was a rule violated, yes or no. If both answers are yes, the defined consequence is applied by the accountable manager, documented with HR, and the matter is closed.

The present practice converts a two-question determination into a multi-person conversation, frequently reaching the Senior Director and the COO. At a sixty to seventy person technician organization this is costly. At the one hundred and twenty person organization the business is planning for, it does not hold. The structure must be able to dispose of routine matters without consuming leadership attention, and that requires the consequence to be defined in advance and the authority to apply it to sit with the manager closest to the event.

Risks

Reversion to existing escalation habits

High

The team escalates because it has always worked. If the Senior Director and the COO continue to answer tactical questions after September 1, the structure changes nothing.

Mitigation. Leadership redirects rather than answers, every time, for the full ninety days. Escalation volume is logged weekly and reviewed with the COO. This risk sits with leadership, not with the team.

Resource Manager authority is not real

High

A resource manager without the standing to require same-day correction becomes an administrator reporting on non-compliance rather than resolving it.

Mitigation. Authority is stated explicitly in the announcement and in the scope document, endorsed by the Senior Services Manager and by HR at the point of announcement rather than incrementally.

Compensation expectations are unresolved at announcement

High

Two roles require adjustment. Announcing a title change while compensation remains open converts a promotion into a grievance.

Mitigation. Phase 0 gates the announcement. Nothing is communicated until compensation is approved.

Associate managers remain in the field

High

The April plan intended to move them into management and it did not happen, because field demand kept pulling them back. Repeating the instruction without changing what schedules them repeats the outcome.

Mitigation. The Resource Manager, not field demand, sets associate manager availability. A protected management allocation is agreed in Phase 0 and reviewed weekly. Where it costs field capacity, that cost is named rather than absorbed.

Senior Services Manager span of control

Medium

Three associate managers, a resource manager, the technical lead group and two subject matter experts approaches the agreed ceiling of four to five direct managers.

Mitigation. The technical leads are largely self-directing and count as a single group against his span rather than as individual reports. Moving them under the associate managers is not the release valve, since that would be read as a demotion by a capable group. If the span becomes unmanageable, the business adds a second services manager.

Associate managers assume full ownership simultaneously

Medium

Three managers take complete people responsibility at the same time, with no staggered ramp.

Mitigation. The Senior Services Manager coaches all three through the same cadence, which is more efficient than sequencing and produces a consistent standard. Weekly manager forum through Phase 2.

Technical transition load on the current services manager

Anticipated questions

These are likely, and several are fair. The plan is stronger for answering them before they are asked.

Why is this reorganization different from the last three?

The service organization has shifted meaningfully three times in two years, so the question is fair and should be answered before it is asked. The difference is that the previous changes moved boxes without changing where decisions terminate, which meant the same behaviour reappeared under new titles within weeks. This plan publishes decision rights on the same day as the chart, and it puts the burden on leadership rather than on the team: the change holds only if the Senior Director and the COO stop answering questions that belong below them. If the leadership group cannot hold itself to that, the honest conclusion is that the business is not yet operating in a way that scales, and that is worth knowing.

Is this a demotion for anyone?

No, and the plan should be presented so that it cannot be read that way. Nothing is being taken from anyone as a judgment on performance. Each move gives a person the whole of one job in place of a fraction of several, and in every case the work being removed is work the person is either not best placed to do or does not want to do.

Does this add headcount?

No. The structure requires no net-new headcount, which is the constraint the executive team has already set. Two compensation adjustments are required. Additional heads follow growth, on the span thresholds set out above, rather than the reorganization.

Will the associate managers actually come out of the field this time?

Only if the scheduling mechanism changes, which is why it is a named decision rather than an instruction. The Resource Manager sets availability, each associate manager carries a protected management allocation, and the allocation is reviewed weekly. Where that costs field capacity, the cost is named rather than absorbed.

What happens to technical questions once the services manager changes?

They continue to reach the Senior Performance Engineer, who remains available for technical direction. The technical leads and associate managers hold considerable capability themselves. What changes is that personnel questions no longer reach him.

Executive bottom line

The talent required to run this business at scale is already funded. The constraint is placement. This plan separates three jobs currently fused inside two roles, assigns each to the person whose capability matches it, and moves technician ownership to the associate managers who already hold the information. It requires no net-new headcount, two compensation adjustments, and a rule that policy violations carry defined consequences rather than convened discussions. Effective September 1, it converts existing cost into growth capacity, ahead of the point at which the current structure stops absorbing the load.
