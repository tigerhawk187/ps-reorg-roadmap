The case for change

Renewance is scaling. The field organization is planned to move from roughly seventy technicians toward one hundred and twenty, and the program portfolio, MVT, LTSA at scale, remote operations, from concept to revenue. That future state is not constrained by talent. It is constrained by placement. Three distinct jobs, technical program leadership, people management and resource management, sit fused inside two roles today, so the business realizes a fraction of each rather than the whole of any. At the same time the support model is inverted at the account level: the portfolio with falling volume carries three people, and the portfolio with rising volume carries one. This plan separates the fused roles, assigns each to the person whose capability matches it, and rebalances account support toward where the volume actually is. The return is growth capacity and margin from a team the business already funds, and executive attention returned to the work only the COO and Senior Director can do.

Scale

Roughly seventy field technicians today, planned toward one hundred and twenty, supporting a footprint that is already nationwide and international.

Programs

MVT, LTSA at scale, remote support and monitoring, technical readiness for regulated markets. None of these mature while the people who would build them are absorbed elsewhere.

Cost to execute

No net-new headcount for the structure. One compensation adjustment, for the Resource Manager seat. One reallocation of work the business already funds, and one rebalancing of account support toward where volume is growing.

Structure without a stated span is a structure that drifts. Three thresholds are agreed, and they are what convert this from a one-time change into a model the business can grow inside.

How growth is absorbed

Growth is absorbed by adding associate managers under the Senior Services Manager. The performance engineering group does not need to scale with technician headcount, because its output is programs rather than coverage. Project management scales with project volume rather than with technician count. When the Senior Services Manager reaches his span, the business adds a second services manager, and regional structure becomes the question worth asking at that point rather than before it.

The Resource Manager seat

The Resource Manager role carries a complete view of how the business runs day to day, which makes it a credible path toward services management. If LTSA becomes a dedicated scope it will need its own services manager, and that is a realistic destination for this role rather than a hypothetical one. The Account Management Group is sized to volume rather than to headcount inherited from history, and it is rebalanced on the same principle when volume shifts again.

LTSA contracts carry obligations that are measured rather than asserted. Availability, capacity, round-trip efficiency and degradation are contractual quantities, and each one is either demonstrated with data or conceded. Renewance is building that capability incidentally today, inside roles whose primary work is something else, and every LTSA the business signs increases the exposure. A performance guarantee the business cannot substantiate is a liability that surfaces at the first dispute or at renewal, whichever comes first.

Performance engineering is the discipline that owns those quantities. It sets the measurement approach, holds the analytics, produces the reporting the asset owner receives, substantiates warranty and guarantee claims against the OEM, and performs root cause when a system underperforms. It is a named function in every mature LTSA organization Renewance will be measured against, and naming it does two things at once. Externally it tells owners and OEMs the capability exists. Internally it gives work that is currently scattered a single home and a single accountable owner. The business needs this for LTSA regardless of any reorganization, which is what makes it the right anchor for the role rather than a label applied to one.

Jordan Larson currently holds two jobs that draw on entirely different capabilities. He is the technical authority the organization consults, and he is the manager of the field services organization. Those two loads compete, and people management wins by default because personnel issues arrive with urgency attached. The work that would compound in value gets whatever hours remain.

The constraint on the new role is as important as its content, and it should be stated plainly: the Senior Performance Engineer carries no people or resource management of the services team. He manages his own three direct reports and nothing beyond them. He does not own planning, he does not own scheduling, he does not own allocation of technicians, and he does not adjudicate personnel matters. Those functions move in their entirety to the Senior Services Manager and the Resource Manager. A personnel question put to him is redirected, not answered, and the redirect is published so the organization expects it. This is a deliberate design constraint rather than an incidental consequence, because a technical role that retains even a partial people-management obligation will be pulled back into it the first week something goes wrong in the field.

The evidence for the placement is unusual, and it is worth stating precisely. Program development is not currently part of Jordan Larson’s role and never has been. The platforms he has built and that the business is now implementing were produced on his own initiative, unassigned, alongside a full people-management load. The business has been receiving that output for free and without having asked for it, which is the clearest available signal of where the capability sits and what it would produce if it were the job rather than the margin around the job. Nothing in this proposal asks whether he can do the work. He is already doing it in the hours the rest of the role leaves behind.

Two placements inside the pillar deserve a word. Training and SOP development moves out of HR and into performance engineering, because the judgment required is technical rather than administrative: what the market demands, which skills the field is short of, and how training is built into a new program early enough that people arrive ready. HR does not have that line of sight and should not be asked to. This does not require a director of training; it requires an owner who can direct it, which is what the role provides. The second is the dotted line to Renewance Connect. The business has capable software development without a consistent source of requirements, which produces tools built on assumption rather than need. Routing product direction through performance engineering supplies that source, and it puts the management of platform development inside the team that understands what the platform is for. The existing development process should be respected rather than compressed, and part of the role is learning it well enough to work within it.

His direct reports reduce to three, and all three are senior individual contributors rather than managers of teams: Renee Zachry as principal technical lead, Gaurav Iyer covering engineering and applied technology, and Dustin Fox owning training and SOP development. Brian Duffey sits on a dotted line for Renewance Connect. Three senior specialists working toward one objective is a supervisory load measured in hours per week, which is what makes the rest of the mandate achievable. Beyond the LTSA performance mandate the role owns the MVT program, the architecture of internal AI and process automation across projects, services, finance and HR, the technical build of remote support and the monitoring center, ticketing system selection and implementation, and the technical readiness assessment required before the business can credibly enter NERC low-to-medium impact markets. It also becomes the vetting function for new initiatives, so that programs stood up under time pressure receive a complete technical specification before they are committed rather than after.

Resource management is presently a residual activity performed by whoever has capacity, which is why it degrades first under load. It is also the single largest consumer of the Senior Services Manager's time. By his own account, resourcing is roughly seventy five percent of everything Clint Dollins does in a working week, which is close to a full time job sitting inside a role that is supposed to be about managing people. Until it is separated, the Senior Services Manager cannot hold the coaching cadence the associate managers need, and the associate managers cannot come out of the field, and nothing else in this plan moves.

Justin Yurchak takes the function. He owns planning and scheduling of the technician workforce and he runs the resourcing call. He owns daily verification that prevailing wage requirements have been met and that timecards are complete and correct, and he owns the follow-through when they are not. He works in close coordination with Patrick Lahlum and the HR team so that compliance is confirmed on a daily cycle rather than reconstructed from six months of records under audit pressure.

Clint Dollins retains the final stamp of approval on resource decisions. This is deliberate and it is not a hedge. The Senior Services Manager remains accountable for where his people go; what he sheds is the mechanical work of building and maintaining the schedule. The test of whether this has worked is not whether Justin Yurchak is busy. It is whether Clint Dollins is holding one-on-ones.

Two things make this work. The first is that planning is a discipline rather than a clerical task. Resourcing decisions have to be made three steps ahead, weighing where a technician is now against where the portfolio needs them in two or three weeks, and the current resourcing call does not look far enough forward to do it. A dedicated planner is how that horizon extends. The second is authority, and this is the condition on which the role succeeds or fails. A planner without the standing to require same-day correction becomes an administrator reporting on non-compliance rather than resolving it. He must be visibly empowered by Clint Dollins and by HR at the point of announcement, not incrementally over the first quarter.

Justin Yurchak's project accounts transfer as part of this move. He does not carry a project portfolio and a resource management function at the same time, because that is the blended arrangement this plan exists to end.

The associate manager designation was created in April to build a leadership layer between the technicians and the service manager. It has not delivered that yet, and the reason is specific: the business has not been able to get the associate managers out of the field. They still carry field assignments, so the management work happens in the margins, and a layer that cannot absorb load is a layer technicians escalate past. The title was granted. The capacity to hold it was not created.

That distinction determines whether this move works, because repeating the April instruction without changing what puts them in the field produces the April result. What changes is the scheduling mechanism. Under this plan the Resource Manager, not field demand, determines associate manager availability, and each associate manager carries a protected management allocation that is scheduled rather than assumed. When a site is short, the answer is a technician or a deferral, not the manager. That has a real cost in field capacity and it should be priced honestly rather than absorbed quietly, which is why the allocation is set in Phase 0 and reviewed weekly by the Senior Services Manager instead of being left to good intentions.

This plan gives each associate manager complete ownership of their technician group. They conduct the one-on-ones. They communicate project assignments and project scope to their technicians. They are the source of direction for their technicians and the destination for their technicians' questions. They are accountable for the conduct and the compliance of their group, and they apply the defined consequence when a rule is broken rather than routing the determination upward. Clint Dollins supervises and coaches them through this transition.

Reachability is a hard requirement of the role rather than a courtesy. If a technician has a problem on a Sunday, that technician reaches their associate manager and gets a person on the phone. This has been intermittent rather than absent, and intermittent is sufficient to break the model, because a technician who cannot reach their manager once will escalate by default from then on. A published standard with a named backup for each associate manager closes it.

One open question sits inside this move. Travel booking can be held centrally by the Resource Manager, which produces consistency and a single point of cost control, or distributed to the associate managers, which reinforces their ownership of their technicians. Both are defensible. The recommendation is central booking under the Resource Manager with associate managers owning the travel plan, which separates the decision about who travels where from the transaction of booking it.

Account support is presently inverted. CATL carries three people against falling volume. Sungrow carries one against volume that is higher than CATL has been at any point, and that is administratively the heaviest account in the business. That is not a judgement about anyone's effort. It is a distribution that made sense when it was built and no longer matches the work.

The CATL arrangement works. It has an account manager who owns the customer and the commercial thread, and an administrator who pulls expenses, prepares work orders for submission, and collects and organises the field reports as they come in day by day. That second function is the reason change orders get caught on CATL before the fact rather than discovered after the invoice. Sungrow has no equivalent. Field execution runs, reports come back incomplete, and the gap is found at the point of billing.

This move merges account support into a single group covering the full portfolio, with four functions named explicitly. Julia Newhall holds Sungrow and the roughly twenty further accounts she already carries. Zoe Thomas holds CATL and hers. Declan Hunt holds his own portfolio on the same terms. The group is defined by the function, not by two OEM names, which is what makes it sized to the real portfolio rather than to the two accounts that prompted the question. Commercial cost modelling and field service execution sit outside the group and interface with it, rather than being absorbed into it.

That last point is the one to hold onto. The existing CATL structure works, and it works partly because it behaves like a small self-contained business sitting inside the project management team, running its own commercial and service threads. That is precisely the blurring between commercial, projects and services this reorganization exists to end. So the group is defined as an operating team with a published interface rather than as a vertical that owns its own commercial function. It gets the coverage without rebuilding the problem.

Julia Newhall stays on Sungrow deliberately. Renewance has already changed the Sungrow point of contact once this year. Changing it again within a few months, on the largest and most administratively demanding account in the portfolio, would spend relationship capital the business has no reason to spend. What changes is not who the customer talks to. It is how much of the back end that person is carrying alone.

Decision rights

A rule that carries a defined consequence does not require a discussion. It requires a determination and a record. The operative test is two questions. Did the individual do it, yes or no. Was a rule violated, yes or no. If both answers are yes, the defined consequence is applied by the accountable manager, documented with HR, and the matter is closed. Authority to decide is not seniority. Service management owns where people go; project management owns the budget and the schedule those people are working against. Neither outranks the other, and where they cannot agree the Senior Director decides rather than the more senior of the two.

The present practice converts a two-question determination into a multi-person conversation, frequently reaching the Senior Director and the COO. At a sixty to seventy person technician organization this is costly. At the one hundred and twenty person organization the business is planning for, it does not function at all. The three most frequent escalations are time punches, expense approvals and rotation availability, and none of the three requires a director to answer it.

The boundary is on direction, not on questions. A project manager may go directly to a technical lead or site lead for a technical fact or a status check, with the associate manager copied so the manager is not surprised by their own site. How work actually arrives

A routing rule that sorts orders into simple and complex was drafted for an earlier version of this plan. Testing it against the record showed it describes a lane that does not exist at Sungrow. Every Sungrow job, regardless of division and regardless of value, is bid through ServiceIQ with a cost model built by commercial. There is no standing rate schedule and no accept-on-receipt path. Recording that here matters, because the plan that follows from it is different from the plan that follows from assuming a simple lane exists.

Intake follows the customer's commercial model, not our preference. Two patterns exist across the portfolio and the larger of the two accounts has access to only one of them.

Flow intake

Order arrives ready to schedule

  • Customer requests a crew for a defined window
  • A quote is issued and approved against known rates
  • Work is scheduled and executed
  • Seen on CATL and on the smaller OEM flow accounts
  • Not available on Sungrow

Low administrative cost per order. The account manager can accept and schedule without commercial building anything. This is the pattern the earlier routing rule assumed applied everywhere.

Bid intake

Every order requires a cost model

  • Every Sungrow job enters the ServiceIQ bidding process
  • A cost model is built for a two thousand dollar job and an eighty thousand dollar job alike
  • Commercial requires technical scope input before it can price
  • Resourcing feasibility must be confirmed before the model closes

High administrative cost per order, applied uniformly. Roughly fourteen to fifteen Sungrow orders arrive per month, and each one consumes commercial, technical and resourcing time before any work is scheduled.

Where an order is ambiguous

The consequence is that Sungrow's constraint is not intake triage. It is cost model throughput. Every model for Sungrow, LG and the flow OEM accounts is presently built by one person in commercial. When that person was last out of the office, the queue was covered by the Sungrow account manager and the handover was difficult. That is a single point of failure on the largest account in the portfolio, and it sits outside this organization's reporting line.

This does not change what this reorganization does. It changes what it must not claim to fix. Rebalancing account support removes the administrative drag around the bid process. It does not widen the bid process itself. The throughput question belongs to commercial, and it is raised here as a dependency rather than solved here as a deliverable.

Risks

Associate managers do not come out of the field on schedule

High

This is the load-bearing move. Every other change depends on it, and it has been attempted before without succeeding. The managers hold titles without ownership today specifically because the business has not been able to release them from field capacity.

Mitigation. The field pull is sequenced first and given its own date rather than being folded into the announcement. Backfill rotations are confirmed before the date, not after. The protected management allocation is scheduled by the Resource Manager rather than left to be taken.

Reversion to existing escalation habits

High

The team escalates because it has always worked. If the Senior Director and the COO continue to answer tactical questions after September 1, the structure changes nothing.

Mitigation. Leadership redirects rather than answers, every time, for the full ninety days. Escalation volume is logged weekly and reviewed with the COO. This risk sits with leadership, not with the team.

The Senior Director keeps the project management team directly

High

This plan exists to remove tactical load from the Senior Director, and it leaves five direct project reports in place. The plan does not add this load, but it does not remove it either, which means one of the two structures generating escalation is untouched. It will not scale with project volume.

Mitigation. Reviewed at ninety days rather than accepted as permanent. The decision rights matrix and the communication protocol are what make it survivable in the meantime, because they keep routine project questions from reaching the Senior Director at all. If project escalation volume does not fall once those publish, the answer is a project management lead, filled internally or recruited.

Resource Manager authority is not real

High

A planner without the standing to require same-day correction becomes an administrator reporting on non-compliance rather than resolving it.

Mitigation. Authority is stated explicitly in the announcement and in the scope document, endorsed by the Senior Services Manager and by HR at the point of announcement rather than incrementally.

Compensation expectations are unresolved at announcement

High

Two roles require adjustment. Announcing a title change while compensation remains open converts a promotion into a grievance.

Mitigation. Phase 0 gates the announcement. Nothing is communicated until compensation is approved.

Commercial cost modelling capacity is a single point of failure

High

Every cost model for Sungrow, LG and the flow OEM accounts is built by one person in commercial. Sungrow alone generates roughly fourteen to fifteen bid-required orders a month. The last coverage event, during planned absence, was handled with difficulty. This sits outside the Projects and Services reporting line but the exposure lands inside it.

Mitigation. Raised to the CFO as a dependency of this plan rather than a deliverable of it. A capacity trigger for an additional proposal manager is being defined in commercial. This plan should not be represented as resolving it.

Senior Services Manager span of control

Medium

Three associate managers, a planner, the technical lead group and two subject matter experts approaches the agreed ceiling of four to five direct managers.

Mitigation. The technical leads are largely self-directing and count as a single group against his span rather than as individual reports. Moving them under the associate managers is not the release valve, since that would be read as a demotion by a capable group. If the span becomes unmanageable, the business adds a second services manager.

Associate managers assume full ownership simultaneously

Medium

Three managers take complete people responsibility at the same time, with no staggered ramp.

Mitigation. The Senior Services Manager coaches all three through the same cadence, which is more efficient than sequencing and produces a consistent standard. Weekly manager forum through Phase 2.

Technical transition load on the current services manager

Medium

Jordan Larson is currently the destination for both technical and personnel questions. Redirecting the personnel half requires the organization to relearn where to go.

Mitigation. Jordan Larson redirects personnel questions to Clint Dollins explicitly rather than answering them, for the full ninety days. Published in the announcement so the redirect is expected.

Approved leave of absence within the project management team

Low

One project manager is on an approved three-month leave of absence during the roadmap window.

Mitigation. Coverage confirmed within the OEM flow portfolio during Phase 2. The Senior Director holds the coverage plan.

Project management is treated as subordinate to service management

Medium

Service management owns resourcing and people, and both functions escalate through the same senior leadership. If project managers come to believe that services input outweighs theirs on their own budgets and schedules, the boundary this plan draws will read as a demotion rather than as clarity.

Mitigation. Stated explicitly in the decision rights principle: neither function outranks the other, project manager input carries equal weight on their own project, and unresolved conflicts go to the Senior Director rather than to the more senior of the two managers.

Anticipated questions

These are likely, and several are fair. The plan is stronger for answering them before they are asked.

Why is this reorganization different from the last three?

The service organization has shifted meaningfully three times in two years, so the question is fair and should be answered before it is asked. The difference is that the previous changes moved boxes without changing where decisions terminate, which meant the same behaviour reappeared under new titles within weeks. This plan publishes decision rights on the same day as the chart, and it puts the burden on leadership rather than on the team: the change holds only if the Senior Director and the COO stop answering questions that belong below them. If the leadership group cannot hold itself to that, the honest conclusion is that the business is not yet operating in a way that scales, and that is worth knowing.

Is this a demotion for anyone?

No, and the plan should be presented so that it cannot be read that way. Nothing is being taken from anyone as a judgment on performance. Each move gives a person the whole of one job in place of a fraction of several, and in every case the work being removed is work the person is either not best placed to do or does not want to do.

Does this add headcount?

No. The structure requires no net-new headcount, which is the constraint the executive team has already set. One compensation adjustment is required, for the Senior Services Manager role. Additional heads follow growth, on the span thresholds set out above, rather than the reorganization.

Will the associate managers actually come out of the field this time?

Only if the scheduling mechanism changes, which is why it is a named decision rather than an instruction. The Resource Manager sets availability, each associate manager carries a protected management allocation, and the allocation is reviewed weekly. Where that costs field capacity, the cost is named rather than absorbed.

What happens to technical questions once the services manager changes?

They continue to reach the Senior Performance Engineer, who remains available for technical direction. The technical leads and associate managers hold considerable capability themselves. What changes is that personnel questions no longer reach him.

Why is Julia Newhall not taking the Resource Manager role?

Two reasons, one about the person and one about the account. She has said directly that she does not want the title or the scope, and she is clear that the customer-facing work is the part of the job she wants to keep. Placing someone in a role they have declined produces an administrator rather than an owner. Separately, Sungrow has already changed its Renewance point of contact once this year. Changing it again within months, on the largest account in the portfolio, spends relationship capital for no operational gain. This plan gives the resource management function to someone who wants it and leaves the customer relationship where it is.

Does one account group rebuild the thing we are trying to get away from?

It would if the group owned its own commercial function, which is what makes the current CATL arrangement uncomfortable. It behaves like a self-contained business sitting inside the project management team. So the group is defined as an operating team with a published interface to commercial and to services rather than as a vertical. Cost modelling stays in commercial. Field execution stays in services. What merges is account administration and customer ownership, which is where the actual gap is.

Why does the project management team not change?

Because nothing in the diagnosis says it should. The team already reports to the Senior Director and has been functioning. What this plan fixes sits in services and in account support, and changing a working reporting line alongside those moves would add disruption without addressing anything. Introducing a leadership layer over the team is a separate decision, deferred rather than declined: there is a real disagreement at leadership level about who should hold it, weighing recent project execution against formal project management experience, and it should be worked through with the individuals concerned rather than settled in a circulated document. Deferring it also avoids a second compensation adjustment.

What happens to Tae Wills?

Nothing changes for him under this option. He remains a project manager on the team, reporting where he reports today, and he retains the decommissioning portfolio, which is the harder portfolio and is already running well. What is not happening is the senior title that was under discussion in the other options, and that is a deferral rather than a decision about him.

Why is software engineering not reporting into performance engineering?

Because the two are peer functions and structuring them as manager and report would misstate that. An earlier version of this plan carried a dotted line and left open the question of a solid line later. Both are removed. What replaces them is a defined collaboration: a standing technical interface that routes field learnings, defects and roadmap input between the two functions, with neither owning the other. Where there is friction between the functions today, the answer is to address it directly rather than to solve it with an org chart.

Should travel booking be centralised under the Resource Manager seat?

No, and an earlier version of this plan recommended it in error. Centralised booking has been tried here before and it failed for a structural reason rather than an execution one: schedules lock late in the week, so booking happens late in the week, and a single owner ends up working Friday nights and weekends while every travel exception in the business lands on one person. That is a bottleneck and a single point of failure. Booking stays distributed across the associate manager teams. The real fix for the pain is upstream, because the reason booking happens late is that schedules lock late, and extending the resourcing horizon to three weeks is exactly what the Resource Manager seat is for.

Executive bottom line

The talent required to run this business at scale is already funded. The constraint is placement. This plan separates three jobs currently fused inside two roles, moves technician ownership to the associate managers who already hold the information, and consolidates account support into one group sized to actual volume rather than to history. It requires no net-new headcount, one compensation adjustment for the Resource Manager seat, and a rule that policy violations carry defined consequences rather than convened discussions. It is sequenced by dependency rather than announced on a date, because the first move, getting the associate managers out of the field, is the one every other move depends on.
